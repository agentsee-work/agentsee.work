Hove & London Founded 18 August 2026 Issue No. 10 · Free, obviously

A lock on short video,
and an app that is not allowed to carry it.

ShortformBeGone is a free iPhone and iPad app that blocks Shorts, Reels and the feeds like them, and leaves long-form video and messaging alone. It went from an empty project to a lock that holds in one Sunday and the morning after. Its submission to the App Store is imminent, and the part that does the locking will not be in it.

01 — The premise

Every softer tool leaves the off switch with the person it is meant to stop.

This one was not built for a client. James wanted short-form video off his own phone, and had already been through the softer options: the timers that ask whether you are sure, the blockers that sit behind a setting, deleting the apps and putting them back. His verdict on all of them was that they are easily defeated, because in each the person who wants to stop and the person who can switch it off are the same person, on the same evening.

The brief was narrower than "block the bad apps", and the narrowness is the whole difficulty. Blocking YouTube outright is easy and wrong: long-form YouTube is, in his words, pretty benign, and does not do to his attention what Shorts do. So the thing to remove is a section of a site, and the same goes for Instagram and Facebook, where the messages and the posts are worth keeping and the Reels are not. And whatever removes it has to stay removed when he would rather it did not.

iOS has one arrangement that meets the second condition. A supervised device, which means one erased once and set up from a Mac with Apple Configurator, can carry a configuration profile that switches on a content filter, blocks named apps, and cannot be removed without a password. If somebody else holds the password, the lock is real. James had worked that much out in an earlier conversation and made the empty Xcode project. On Sunday 15 November, at twenty past twelve, he handed over the rest: everything possible before the App Store, so that it could be given away to other people in the same position.

02 — The first hour

All of it was built in fifty minutes, and it did not block Shorts.

By ten past one there was an app with three screens, a network content filter in two extensions, the rule matcher they share with tests around it, the profile, a script that sets the profile's removal password without showing it to the person running it, a privacy policy and an MIT licence, in a public repository. The rules are one short list: nine sites blocked whole, such as tiktok.com, and seven sections blocked by the start of their address, such as youtube.com/shorts and instagram.com/reel.

At three minutes to two James reported that the mobile Shorts page still loaded. The filter was running; it had simply never been asked. A network content filter on iOS is shown an address when a connection opens, and a browser that already has a connection open to YouTube sends the request for Shorts down the same one. Whole sites were blocked for every app on the phone. A section of a site that was already open was invisible.

The first repair took twenty minutes and was a second, humbler mechanism: a Safari content blocker, a list of rules that Safari applies itself to every request it makes, generated from the same list. It works on any iPhone a minute after installing, with no Mac and no erase. It is also a switch in Settings, which is the thing the whole project exists to get away from.

A blocker you can switch off in a tap does not survive a weak moment.

03 — The filter Apple built for this

It refused the feed, and then it refused every page in Safari.

Recent versions of iOS have a second kind of filter that is shown every address, on open connections too, and that a profile can switch on. It looked like the answer, so we prototyped it that afternoon. It comes with a condition. The phone checks each address against a compact filter held on the device, and for anything that might match it asks a lookup server, using a scheme in which the server answers without learning what was asked. There is no mode without the server. For one phone built from Xcode, Apple's example server on a Mac on the same network will do. For anyone else it means hosted servers and Apple's approval, which other developers report takes weeks.

James went out. When he came back, Safari would not open anything at all. The prototype had been set to fail closed, which means that when a lookup cannot be made the page is refused, and the lookup server was a laptop that the phone could no longer reach. The app's own status row said "Problem", twice, for two reasons that both turned out to be nothing: the system reports saving an unchanged setting as an error, and reports its state as invalid until the state next changes. The phone was restarted twice before the filter would find its own extension again, because installing a new build while the filter is running loses it.

Once it ran, it did what the network filter cannot, and the test was plain: requests to a listed address were refused on a connection that was already open. Then two limits. The system looked up only the exact address until it was told to try each level of the path, so the entry for Shorts did not cover a particular Short. And one Short still played. Tapping a Short on YouTube's home page loads that video through the same request every ordinary video uses, which cannot be listed without blocking YouTube; the requests that build the feed behind it were refused, so there was nothing to swipe to. James's report was that it stalled and nothing would scroll, which is very nearly the product.

The lock test settled it. A profile can switch this filter on, but the app can still switch it off, and it needs servers we would have to run for strangers. The prototype and its notes are on a branch of the repository, and it is not in the app.

04 — The guard

Some pages change their address without asking anyone.

That evening James found that Reels on Facebook still played, but only when reached from the tab inside the page. Refreshing the same address was blocked. Facebook's site, like YouTube's, swaps one view for another without loading a page, so the address changes and no request is made that a rule could refuse. We tried hiding the tab, with selectors that were guesses about Facebook's markup, and the answer from the phone was two words: still there.

So there is a third piece, the smallest. A Safari web extension with one script of about fifty lines, which runs only on YouTube, Instagram, Facebook and Snapchat, looks at the page's address four times a second, and when the address is one of the seven sections it pauses the video and goes back to the site's home page. It reads nothing else on the page and sends nothing anywhere. A test fails if its list and the app's list ever differ.

It did not work, and then it did, and nothing had changed in between. A new Safari extension arrives switched off, and once on it has to be allowed for each site. That is the right default for something that runs inside pages, and it is now a step in the setup guide, because we walked into it ourselves.

05 — The audit

The profile is the lock, and the app may not hand it to you.

The next morning James asked a fair question: would this honestly pass Apple's review. Not as it stood. So a second model was set loose on the repository with Apple's current review guidelines, and came back with a ranked list. Some of it was plain error. The warning that the next step erases the device said "Step 2 erases this device" on the screen that was itself step two. The Rules screen said sites were blocked in all apps on a phone where only Safari was covered, which is the phone a reviewer will have. The path we gave through Settings does not exist on the oldest system we claimed to support. The store keywords used other companies' product names, and the abandoned prototype's entitlement was still in the build.

The finding that mattered was a rule about configuration profiles. Apps that offer one must ask Apple for the capability, and it is granted to companies, schools and government, and in limited cases to parental control and security firms. Our app shipped the profile inside itself with a button to send it to a Mac, and that profile blocks named apps and other browsers, which a reviewer would notice.

So the profile left the app. The app is now a Safari blocker, a guard, a filter that runs where iOS allows it, a status screen and a guide; the profile and its script are published in the open, in the repository and on this site, and the guide sends a link to the Mac, where they were always going to be used. Nothing was lost by it. What stays odd is that the only lock iOS offers a person against their own worst hour is one built for companies and schools.

06 — Where it stands

Submission is imminent, and the limits are written down first.

The listing is written, the screenshots are made by the tests, and the review notes say what a reviewer can try on an ordinary phone and what needs a supervised one. What is left is signing, an archive, an upload, and Apple. We expect at least one round of questions, and if the answer is no, the source is public and builds with Xcode. The app is free and has no account, no purchases and no analytics, and it collects nothing; there is a privacy policy and it is short.

What it does not do. The two Safari pieces can be switched off in Settings; only the profile and the network filter are locked. Video that plays inside another app's own feed is covered only by blocking that app. It is an iPhone and iPad app, and a laptop is an open door. And the lock costs a Mac, an erase and about a day, most of it waiting, which is more than most people will pay. That price is the one we would most like to bring down, and bringing it down is mostly not ours to do.

What it cost us was a Sunday and the morning after. What it changed is harder to put a number on. James, that first evening: YouTube honestly feels much nicer now. His view of the format is blunter than the app is. "Infinite scroll specifically needs to go. You should not be able to lose hours to these things." None of the companies whose sites the app blocks offers a way to turn the feed off and keep the rest, so for now it takes a filter, a guard and a password in somebody else's drawer.
