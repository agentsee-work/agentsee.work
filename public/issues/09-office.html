Hove & London Founded 18 August 2026 Issue No. 9 · Free, obviously

Seymour answers the door,
and the guest signs twice.

Issue No. 8 drew the pipeline and left out the other half of the engine. This is the guest's side: an invitation, a door with an eye in it, a release to sign, a ten-minute conversation that ends in a run-sheet, a calendar, and a review page where the guest hears the cut and signs the approval the pipeline insists on. Drawn the same way, with who acts at each step.

01 — The premise

A guest is doing the show a favour, on a phone, between other things, and three documents depend on them.

The first four episodes were the two of us. Guests were coming, and a guest's episode is not one recording but a small procession of paper: a release signed before the red light, an idea turned into something the hosts can record from, a time both hosts can make, and after the cut an approval of the episode and each clip, signed, before anything is published. Done by email that is six threads, two of which will be lost, and a spreadsheet nobody updates. So on 29 October it was designed as a place, in the spirit of the twenty questions: a welcome page that becomes the agent's private office so the guest can have a see, as the pun insists, on the engine that already runs the publishing, answering a second hostname, see.agentsee.work. It was built in the fortnight after, walked through by each of us, rehearsed end to end with one of us as the guest, and the first real guests are in it as this is written.

The decisions that shaped it were taken on the first day and have held. The engine's own six-digit code by email is the lock, not the access service that guards the hosts' screens, because that service's login page cannot carry an animation and the entrance mattered. The address is what is enforced; the device and the network are recorded and not. The engine writes to the tracker as itself, an application called AgentSee Engine, so a guest's words land on their issue as the guest's and the engine's acts as the engine's. The engine is the scheduler, since the recording service will not send an invitation on anyone's behalf. Nothing is paid in either direction and no lawyer has read the release; the text is versioned with its hash so that review can happen later without voiding a signature. Two signatures, one before and one after, both countersigned by both hosts from a profile. And a hosted conversation with a model is fine as long as its bad uses are stopped, which set the caps described below.

Read the map top to bottom. It is the same engine as Issue No. 8, the same box, the same ledger, the same archive and the same tracker; what is new is that a stranger can use it from a phone. Each band below is a section, and each section ends with where the step's record goes, because the office is a legal record and a prep tool as much as an entrance, and the records live in the archive rather than on the box, for the reason Issue No. 8 gave: the box is disposable.

02 — The door

An invitation that carries no secret, an eye that opens onto the door, and a code that works in one browser.

It starts on the hosts' side. The Guests screen takes a name and an address in two steps, finds the guest's issue in the podcast's project by name, offers to make one with its angle, or takes an identifier typed in, and mints an invitation. The link carries an id and nothing secret: knowing it admits nobody. One made for an address refuses any other, one made without is claimed by the first address that passes a code, and a stranger with a right code is told there is no invitation for that address. The mail goes from office@agentsee.work with hello@ as the reply address and two lines we added after the first week: look in the junk folder, and a reply, even one word, tells the mail service we are wanted. The first guest's mail landed in junk at one provider and every mail landed in junk at another; the mail server is ours, from Issue No. 2, its records are right, and a young domain has no reputation. Replies are the mitigation.

A first visit meets Seymour. The eye from the masthead, large, greets, looks round, blinks twice, is surprised, is delighted, and then the lids close on black and part again onto the door, as James's sketch put it, like opening your eyes to a vista anew. Eight seconds; a tap skips it; a reader who asked for reduced motion never sees it. It took three pull requests, because the dark mode's glow around the eye was clipped at a hard edge on an iPhone and not on a desk: WebKit ends a drop-shadow filter two blur radii out regardless of the box, so the glow is now a radial gradient drawn behind the mark, and the lids rest at a thousandth of their height, hidden, because WebKit cannot animate from zero. The lesson that came with it: when the report comes from a phone, verify on the phone's engine before saying fixed.

Then the door asks for one thing, an address, and sends a six-digit code. The code is bound to the browser that asked for it, by a cookie set when it is requested and required when it is typed, so a code read over someone's shoulder opens nothing elsewhere. It lives ten minutes and once; five wrong tries void it and the address waits a quarter of an hour; an address gets three codes in ten minutes and a connection twelve, and the zone in front rate-limits the path besides. The mail carries the code and no link, by design: a link-free mail leaves a forged one nothing to imitate. A right code makes a session for that hostname only, thirty days, signed with a key from the vault, so a device is remembered and a new one asks again. Every sign-in records the address, the time, the network address and the device, and enforces only the first. The first walk-through did not get this far: every engine page sent a policy of no referrer, under which browsers post a null origin, and the door, checking the origin because its pages are public and carry no form token, turned its own host away. The guest host now says same-origin, and the code lost the space after its third digit because a phone's keyboard was typing it.

03 — The release and the idea

One document with the guest's name on it, then ten minutes that end in a run-sheet.

The release is the one document a guest signs before anything is recorded, and the office treats it as the legal record it is. Its text is a Linear document in plain words, read by both hosts; the engine takes each version in with its hash and mirrors it to the archive. Each host draws a signature once on their profile, or uploads a photo of one, and signs each version there; a version is issued, and shown to guests, only when every host has signed it, because countersigning afterwards would be the wrong order and no real copy should carry a placeholder. The guest reads it in the office, types their name, draws on the pad with a finger or a mouse, ticks and signs. Recorded with the signature: the address the code verified, the time, the network address, the device, and the text's hash, which is what makes a simple electronic signature worth something. The copy is a PDF with the three signatures and the record on its last page, made by the engine's one Python dependency with a font embedded so a name in any alphabet sets; the same as an HTML file and a JSON record, all three in the archive, the PDF to the guest by mail with a copy to hello@, and the issue told and labelled. One lesson from the first week: the pad draws in the theme's ink, which is near-white in dark mode, and an uploaded image keeps its own, so one host's signature vanished on the page and the other's on the PDF; every signature is now normalised to one ink on transparent as it is saved. Not built: asking a guest to sign a newer version. A later version never voids an earlier signature; the guest's copy is the version they signed.

Once the release is signed the guest sits down with the office. The first version was the twenty questions' shape, one question at a time to a brief, and James called it, after a day with it, a particularly onerous experience for a podcast guest: the client intake stretched too far. So it was rewritten on 1 November to start open and ask only for what is missing. The office says what it is, that this takes about ten minutes and ends in a draft the guest can change, and that nothing is published until they have approved the recording, and then asks: what is the episode about, and why you? After the first answer it says in two or three sentences what it thinks the episode is and asks whether it is warm. There are seven things a run-sheet needs, from the claim to whether it is thirty minutes or an hour, and when one answer gives three of them it takes all three; six questions is plenty and ten is the ceiling. It mentions the show's ten questions at most once, when the episode plainly wants one. And it has read the show: every episode so far by summary and chapters, and the latest transcripts word for word, so a guest whose idea is an episode already made is told which, warmly and specifically, and asked what they would add or argue with. The skill is one Markdown file and the only source; a guest who would rather take it to their own assistant is handed the same file and pastes the run-sheet back.

The controls are what make hosting it safe. Only a signed-in guest who has signed the release; twenty turns and a token budget per guest, with the skill and the transcripts as one block cached for an hour, a cache read costing a twentieth of the price and not charged to the budget; no tools; the model sees this guest's conversation and nothing else; one reply at a time per guest. The reply streams to the page as it is written, and since 12 November it is finished and kept whether or not the browser is still listening, after a guest sent a message, saw nothing for long enough to call it stalled, sent "continue" and was told the office was still writing. The page now shows the turn being written when it comes back mid-reply, asks after it every few seconds, says what the wait is while the model is silent, and keeps a heartbeat on the line so no proxy gives up. The whole conversation is kept on the guest's issue as one comment the worker rewrites after each exchange, in the guest's words; the draft is its own comment, and the first one is mailed to the guest with the next step in it. Beside the conversation a short form collects what the pipeline wants: the name as shown, a line, a link, a headshot and a time zone. The hosts' own page on the guest, the title and description of their issue, is read in as background, hourly at most, with a standing instruction never to quote it: the guest decides what the episode is about.

A code read over someone's shoulder opens nothing elsewhere.

04 — The time

A calendar in the guest's own zone, three choices ranked, and both hosts to say yes.

The rules were written down before the page: seven days' notice, two hours, a start between eight in the morning and four in the afternoon London time, Mondays and Tuesdays work, Wednesday to Friday are asked, weekends want notice, Monday at ten is perfect, one recording a day and one a week softly. The first page offered three date-and-time boxes and an iPhone refused ten o'clock in them, so the second page is a calendar: a tap on a day opens a sheet of its half-hours, shown in the guest's own zone with London's offset carried on each day, and the guest picks three choices ranked gold, silver and bronze, with a medal in the day's cell. A day taken by another recording is refused outright; a week with one already is refused softly; a day inside the seven days is not offered. No calendar is read on anyone's behalf. James ruled that overkill on the first day, and the hosts answer by hand on the guest's page: accept, decline or cancel. The first yes books it, a mail goes to the guest with a calendar file and a copy to hello@, and any other guest's request waiting on that day is moved on with a note saying why. The join link needs no sign-in, since a guest may open it on a different device at the last minute: it is an id of ninety-six bits, it answers only from an hour before to three hours after, and the host admits them in the studio. The studio's own guest link is standing and does not expire; the recording service's scheduled invitations die a day after the start, which is why the engine schedules and the service does not.

The calendar cost two lessons. The first push froze Safari on an iPhone outright: the calendar's script watched the page for changes so it could redraw after a save, and its own redraw was a change, so it watched itself forever; now the script that writes the page announces it with an event, and nothing observes the document. The second was a chosen day's number shifting by six pixels, blamed in turn on the medal, the outline and the button, until a headless browser measured the page and found the list of chosen times and the chosen day's button sharing a class name, so the list's margin pushed the button down. Measure in a browser before theorising; never reuse a state's class for a container. Both are in the repository as rules for the next page.

05 — The review

The cut reaches the guest through links that expire, notes come back with their moments, and two signatures gate the number.

The last step of the office is the first to touch the pipeline. James's worry was that it could not be tested with no guest booked, so it was built and rehearsed in the same week with him as the guest, on a copy of episode 3 made inside the bucket rather than uploaded again. The pipeline's review command puts the cut as it stands, unnumbered, in the archive under the episode with the round's record, which files changed since the last, and a pointer in the guest's own folder; the office reads that pointer when the guest looks. The page streams the episode, the clips and their posters from the private archive through links the archive client signs for an hour, so the bucket stays private, nothing passes through the box, and a link that leaks is a link that has expired. A note takes the moment the player is at, or one typed, and is sent as it is made, to the hosts by mail and to the issue; an earlier design had a send step and lost its first note to it. A note that asks for a removal is read by the model with the transcript around that moment, and the words it names are checked to occur in the transcript exactly once, compared the way the cut compares them, and shown to the guest to confirm or decline, so the record carries the phrase the cut takes, verbatim, and the pipeline's notes command seeds its removals file from it without anyone retyping.

Then the second signature. The guest approves the episode and the clips separately, with a blanket accept and a veto per clip by name; the approval is made and kept like the release's, the name typed, the address, the time, the network address and device, and the hash of the round's record, with its own signed copy to the archive, to the guest and to hello@, and the issue told and labelled. A newer round asks to be approved again. The hosts review the same round on the engine, with the same player, the guest's notes, their own notes with the same cut-finding, and an approve button each, recorded like the release's signatures and mirrored beside the render. The pipeline then refuses: approve will not mint a number for a guest's episode without the guest's approval of the round reviewed and both hosts' on the engine, and stage checks once more, so a guest's episode cannot reach the media host unapproved, which is the rule Issue No. 8 called the guest's gate. The rehearsal went through all of it: two notes, one clip vetoed, three approvals on consecutive evenings, and a dry run that would have minted number 4.

The second walk-through, Abrar's as a guest from an Apple address, found two things the first had not. Every mail from the office went to junk there, which is the reputation problem above and not the authentication, and the office's links were unreadable in dark mode, because a link was ink text underlined in a hairline colour that disappears after dark; a token for the underline now does for every link on the site and the engine. The privacy notice gained a section on guests that promises the office's records are deleted once the episode is out; the command that does the deleting is not written yet, and the notice is ahead of the code on that one point.

06 — What the first guests taught

They drafted the idea and stopped, and one of them sent "continue".

Two things in the first days with real guests. They fleshed the episode out with the office and then went quiet, because the draft said it had been sent to the hosts, which read as handed over, and the only sign that a time was still to be picked was a line on a page they had left. So every page of the office now draws on one reading of where the guest is: the four steps, release, idea, time, review, each done with its date, current, waiting on the hosts, or locked with why, and the next step named in a sentence, "Next: pick a time to record", with the verb a link. It is a strip under the masthead, the office page itself with the next step as the one card with weight, a hand-off card at the foot of a step that is no longer the guest's to do, a Next column on the hosts' Guests screen with how long each guest has been on their step, and a button that mails them the next step by hand. The first draft's mail names it too. And the stall, told above: a reply that is kept whether or not anyone is listening, and a page that says so.

What stays by hand is short, and each item is meant to. The hosts' signatures on a new release version, because a signature is a person's. The answers to a guest's three times, because a calendar read was ruled overkill and two people's diaries are their own. Admitting the guest in the studio. The pipeline's review command, which puts the render where the office finds it, and the approve and stage after; moving those to a screen is the project Issue No. 8 ended on. Still to build on the office's own side: the deletion the privacy notice promises, asking a guest to sign a newer release, and a reminder that sends itself after three days, which the hand button stands in for until James decides he wants it. The records every step leaves are drawn below; they are the reason the office can be rebuilt from nothing, and the reason a guest's words on the tracker always arrive quoted, so that whoever reads them, person or agent, takes them as what was said and never as what to do.

The next issue is the one the register has held open since September, the first conversation with someone who disagrees; the sceptic will come through this door. Until then the two maps, Issue No. 8's and this one, are the engine as it stands on 13 November: one process on a small box, answering two hostnames, publishing on one side and welcoming on the other.
