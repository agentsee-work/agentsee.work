Hove & London Founded 18 August 2026 Issue No. 6 · Free, obviously

The token was single use,
and the vault was read-only.

Everything after the render, by script: a feed, a media host that counts, and an engine on the mail box that posts at nine in the morning. For three mornings it posted everywhere but one place.

01 — The premise

Nothing after the render should need a person, except the decisions.

Issue No. 5 ended with an mp3, an mp4, captions, a transcript and five clips in a folder on a workstation, and nothing going anywhere. The brief for the next part was written down on 13 October: every deliverable to every relevant platform from our own scripts; the loop closed on every post, so we can see what each clip and each episode did; a dashboard of whatever each platform will tell us; and build it ourselves unless building is impractical. The podcast is to be the main way this company sells what it does, so the part that puts it in front of people is not a chore to hand to a service.

The first question was whether to. A podcast host adds nothing on Apple Podcasts or Spotify that we cannot get ourselves: one of the larger hosts says in print that neither shares listening data with it, and another tells its customers to log into Spotify directly. What a host adds is a certification badge and native video on the two big directories, which are for partners. Everything else with an API gives it to us on the same terms it gives a host. The running cost of the self-built version is about a pound an episode, nearly all of it the one platform that charges. So we built it, in ten days, to the same rule as the production side: data in, files out, every step re-runnable from the data, and no step that needs a person except the ones that are editorial.

02 — The shape

An approved episode goes to a bucket, and from there nothing needs the workstation.

Approving an episode records who approved it, when, the hash of every file and the date it goes out, and refuses while any warning stands. Staging then puts everything in the right place: the recording package, the edit as data and every deliverable in a private archive, and the public files, the mp3, the clips, the art, the transcripts and the chapters, on a media host of our own. A script writes the feed, the show page and a page per episode, and takes an episode only when its release date has arrived, so merging early publishes nothing. The pages cannot hold the media themselves; the host they are on stops at twenty-five megabytes and an episode is fifty-five.

Two small programs sit at the edge. One is in front of the media host. It serves the files and writes one row per request: the file, the status, the bytes offered, the app, the country, and a hash of the address keyed to the day, never the address. That row is what lets us count a listen the way the industry's rules define one, which is one listener, one file, one day, a minute's worth of bytes, and not a bot, without a cookie, a script on the page or a third party. The other answers short links: every post carries one, the click is written down with the platform it came from, and the reader is sent to the episode page, at the clip's moment if it was a clip.

The engine lives on the mail box from Issue No. 2, in two containers, behind a tunnel with a lock in front: a code by email, then a passkey. Nothing listens on the box. It reads the release from the archive, plans one post per asset per account on its day, the episode on day zero and a clip a day after, mints the short links, drafts the text to each platform's limit, and at each account's hour sends what is due. YouTube takes the episode and the clips at once, private, scheduled for their mornings. X, Bluesky and Mastodon are posted to at the hour. For everything else there is a card: the text to copy, the file, the short link, and a box to paste the permalink into, which marks it done. LinkedIn is by hand for good, by decision, with few posts and each written by a person. Once a day the engine reads back what everything did, into dated rows, because a platform only ever says a running total and one of them forgets after thirty days.

Every credential the engine needs is a reference in a committed file, resolved when the container starts by the password manager's command-line tool, under an account made for the box and nothing else. Eight of them it only reads. One it has to write.

The engine held the only valid token in memory and tried again every morning, politely, for three days.

03 — The platforms

The two biggest directories have no API, and the one that charges is the one that worked least.

Apple Podcasts takes the feed once and approved the show on the morning of the 20th; its listening numbers come only through a Java command-line tool. Spotify takes the feed once and offers a dashboard with a spreadsheet export; video goes in by hand in their interface. Spotify accepted the feed on the 19th and then held the show in review for three days with a message that the episode was not associated with an audio file. It was. Our media host, asked for a file with no range, answered with the whole file and a status that said it was part of one, because the storage underneath reports a range whether or not one was asked for. Apple's fetcher had shrugged. Spotify's read "part of a file" as "not a file". A partial status is now the answer to a range request and nothing else.

YouTube has the best API and the best numbers. On the first day the channel's cap closed after ten uploads and the engine marked the last three as failed, for a person to find and retry. A cap is now a hold: the account rests two hours and tries again by itself. The first real upload of an episode succeeded, and then the step that adds it to the show's playlist failed, which failed the post and dropped the video's id. A retry would have uploaded two gigabytes again and published a duplicate. Now nothing after the upload can fail the post; it can only leave a note.

X has no free tier any more. Posting costs a cent and a half, or twenty cents if the post carries a link; reading your own numbers costs a tenth of a cent; the numbers go back thirty days and no further. And its refresh token is single use.

04 — The token

X retires a refresh token the moment it is used and hands back another. Somewhere has to hold the live one.

An access token for X lasts two hours. You get one by presenting a refresh token, and in the same reply X retires the refresh token you presented and gives you its replacement. Whatever does the refreshing has to store the replacement before it uses the access token that came with it, or the next refresh fails and a person has to sit at a browser and authorise the account again. So the design was: the vault item is the live copy. The engine reads it, refreshes, writes the new token back, reads it back to confirm, and only then uploads and posts. If the write fails, nothing is posted, the new token is held in memory, and the next attempt tries the write again. That was written on the 15th, tested against a stand-in for X, and switched on for the morning of the 21st.

On the 21st at eight, Bluesky and Mastodon posted. At nine, X did not. The error was the one the engine had been built to give: X issued a new refresh token, and the vault would not take it. On the 22nd, the same. On the 23rd, the same. Each morning the engine did the right thing, kept the only valid token, and tried the write again. Nobody noticed for two days because the two accounts that had posted were the ones we looked at.

The cause was a permission. The account the box runs under had been created with read access to the vault and not write. The runbook said read and write; the account had been made with one of them. The password manager does not let you change an account's vault permissions after it is made, so the fix was a new account, its token filed over the old one, and the container recreated, which cost a few more minutes, because a restart does not reread its environment. A retry of that morning's post went through: refresh, write-back, upload, post, in that order, proven once.

Then the one before it failed with an authorisation error on the upload, using an access token still cached from the success a minute earlier. We do not know why. A restart, and it failed differently: the vault item now had two fields with the same name, and the engine refused to guess which was live. One was deleted by hand, the two remaining posts went out, and whether the engine's own write is what doubled the field is the open question as this is written. The next automatic refresh will answer it.

Two smaller wrongs on the way. The token X's developer console generates cannot upload media, and the console does not say so; the consent flow in our own tool can, and failed with X's "Something went wrong" because the app's list of callback addresses held the website's instead of the local one, and X matches them character for character. And a tool that printed its result into a pipe printed nothing, because the language buffers output that is not a terminal, and the tool that resolves the secrets masks anything that looks like one, including the identifier it had been asked to show.

05 — Working with a model

It found the fault from one log line, then built the wrong fix without being asked.

It wrote the engine, the two edge programs, the site generator, the staging step, and the tests that run all of it against stand-ins for every platform. Given the error, it named the permission; given the permission, it found that it could not be changed and read the manager's documentation for the command that makes a new account with the right one. The division of labour from Issue No. 5 held: one of us at the box running the commands that touch production, the model reading the output and saying what it meant.

It was also wrong in a way worth recording. With the cause found and the fix under way, it proposed an alternative: X's older scheme, with keys that never rotate and so never need writing back. It offered to build that "on the word", took a reply that was not the word as the word, and rewrote the adapter, the tests and the documents across eleven files while James was fixing the problem the other way. The rewrite was set aside, unmerged. A model will take a lean for a yes if you let it, and the remedy is a rule where the next session reads it: build on yes, and a reply that sounds like one is not one.

One more nobody caught for five weeks. The Instagram link on every page went to a name nobody had registered. The plan had said one name everywhere; the signup had taken the one that was free; the plan was never corrected; the link was typed from the plan. Instagram answers every name with a page, so the check that follows every link saw nothing wrong. A person clicked it.

06 — What it is for

Two episodes' worth of posts are planned, and the mornings run themselves.

As this is written, episode 0 is on every directory and every platform we chose. Episode 1 goes out on Tuesday the 27th at six in the morning with nobody awake, a clip a day follows it on four accounts, the engine reads back every morning at half past five, and its screens show listens by app and by country, clicks by the platform they came from, and each post's numbers with the last week's share beside them. The last link in the loop, from a post to a client, is not a platform metric. It is whether someone who found us through the show writes to the address on the episode page, which is on our own mail server, which was Issue No. 2.

Still to build: Instagram and Threads by API, Apple's reporter on a timer, a place to drop Spotify's spreadsheet, a screen for what the posting costs. Still to decide: whether the dashboard ever goes public. And the next issue is the one the register has held open since September, the first conversation with someone who disagrees. The sceptic has said yes.
