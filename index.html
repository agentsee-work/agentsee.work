01 — The premise

Every software agency that has ever existed was organised around one scarce thing: engineering time.

The account manager protected it. The delivery manager sequenced it. The business analyst translated into it. The estimate priced it. The change request defended it.

The org chart, the rate card and the statement of work are all scaffolding built around a single constraint — and everything an agency sells is shaped by the cost of the thing it was protecting.

We don't think that makes agencies obsolete. We think it makes most of their structure obsolete, and we would rather find out what replaces it by building one than by writing another post about it.

The constraint moved.

So this is the whole thing, from the ground up: what the roles are, what you buy, what it costs, who is accountable, and what it is actually like to be a person doing this work every day.

We are at the very earliest stage of that. This document is the first thing we have published, and it exists to be argued with rather than agreed with.

02 — What we actually don't know

These are not rhetorical. We don't have the answers, and we are suspicious of anyone currently selling them.

  1. If writing the code is no longer the expensive part, what is the client actually buying?
  2. What replaces the estimate? If you can see the working software before you commit, do you pay to go to production rather than paying for the sprint?
  3. What is a product manager when the specification is executable?
  4. Who is accountable for a system that nobody typed?
  5. How do you run acceptance testing on something produced faster than a person can read it?
  6. Where does the next generation of senior engineers come from, if the junior tier that grew them no longer exists?
  7. What does taste mean when volume is free?
  8. Do engineers still want the job? What is it now — author, reviewer, director, or something we don't yet have a word for?
  9. What is the honest rate card, and would you publish yours?

03 — How we're going about it

We're going to spend a long time asking before we spend any time selling.

In public, on the record

Streams and a podcast with people who run agencies, buy from agencies and work inside them — including the ones who think this premise is nonsense. The discovery is the content. It isn't marketing for a thing that already exists.

Built alongside you

Built with you rather than delivered to you — in the room while it's made, not on the far side of a statement of work. That means partners bought into the approach and clear-eyed that it's a risk, with a share of the reward that reflects having taken it.

Discovery first, then partners

The discovery isn't a formality we're hurrying through to get to the selling. When we're clearer, we'll start with a small number of partners — and we'll publish what we charge and how we arrived at it. Our own tooling gets run on us first; if it doesn't hold up there, we've no business charging for it.

Radical honesty is an easy thing to put on a landing page, so here is ours: we have no proven model, no client list and no product. We have two engineers, a domain name and a strong suspicion.
